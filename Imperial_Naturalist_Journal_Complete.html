Editor's Foreword

On the Nature of This Volume

The creatures catalogued in the following pages represent the most comprehensive survey of New World fauna yet assembled under Imperial auspices. They have been gathered from field reports, survivor testimonies, expedition journals, and in several cases from accounts recovered from the personal effects of naturalists who did not return. Where multiple accounts contradict one another, the editors have exercised judgement. Where accounts agree but strain credulity, the editors have elected to print them anyway — because in the New World, the improbable has a persistent habit of being true.

The reader is advised that this volume is not arranged by taxonomic order, nor by threat level, nor by geographic distribution, as no system yet devised by Imperial scholarship adequately captures the full strangeness of what lives across the ocean. Entries are instead arranged by rough category, moving from common wildlife to apex predators, from manageable nuisances to things that should not exist but evidently do.

The sketches are drawn from life where possible. The anecdotes are reproduced verbatim. The editors accept no responsibility for what the reader does with this information, but strongly recommend they keep their gloves on, their eyes on the canopy, and their campfire attended at all times.

— Professor Aldous Fenwick-Crane, Chair of Colonial Zoology
Imperial Academy of Natural Sciences, Third Floor, Room 14
(The room with the good locks)

Entry the First

Razorwing Bat

Chiroptera ferrum — Small colonial predator, cave-dwelling

Armour-Piercing Cave Dweller Colonial

The Razorwing Bat is a colony predator native to the cave networks and cliff faces of the New World, and the first creature in this volume to demonstrate a principle the reader will encounter repeatedly: that in the New World, things which look familiar are reliably worse than they appear. It looks like a bat. It is a bat. It is also, along the trailing edge of each wing, armoured with a naturally-occurring metalite alloy that the creature grows from mineral deposits in its diet, and which it sheds and regrows across its lifetime with the casual indifference of a man changing his boots.

A solitary Razorwing is a manageable nuisance — roughly equivalent to an angry kitchen knife with wings and opinions. A colony of thirty descending at dusk is something else. The metallic edges produce a distinctive sound when a flock is in motion: a rapid, high-frequency ringing, like someone running a finger around the rim of a hundred glass goblets simultaneously. Settlers who have heard this sound and survived consistently report that they heard it approximately one second before the bats arrived.

"We had been warned about the cliff settlements. We had not been warned with adequate specificity. Our guide said 'watch for the bats.' What he did not say was that the bats in question could shear through standard-issue vambraces on a diving pass, and that they coordinate. They timed it. Three of them hit Constance simultaneously from different angles. I watched her armour come apart. She survived. The armour did not." — Surveyor Aldous Renn, Third Colonial Expedition, personal journal

When three or more Razorwings focus on a single target, the coordination of their strikes — seemingly instinctive rather than planned — creates a disorienting cross-pattern of attacks that impairs even experienced fighters' ability to track incoming threats. Settlers near cliff-face encampments report chronic livestock losses that no amount of netting has fully resolved, as the bats learn netting patterns within a single season.

Field Note — Deterrence

Loud percussion instruments, bright light, and smoke from pine resin are the most effective deterrents. Iron mesh rather than rope or cloth netting. Do not sleep beneath overhangs in cliff settlements without a roof between you and the rock. The metalite edge does not discriminate between livestock and naturalists.

Entry the Second

Ashback Lizard

Lacerta cinerem — Small, volcanic scavenger

Blinding Fire Immune Mining Pest Burning

The Ashback Lizard is the creature most commonly encountered in and around New World mining operations, smelting camps, and volcanic-fringe settlements, and the one most frequently underestimated by newcomers who note its modest size and conclude it poses no serious threat. This conclusion is the last conclusion a surprising number of them reach before the ash spit makes further conclusions difficult.

Its hide has been hardened by a lifetime spent in thermal environments — pressing against hot stone, sleeping in ash beds, sheltering in active forges when it can manage it. The result is a natural armour equivalent to a respectable suit of leather plate, which makes conventional pest-control methods frustratingly ineffective. Its stealth capability, combined with this hardening, means it regularly infiltrates equipment stores, food caches, and sleeping quarters before anyone notices it is there.

"The foreman warned us about the Ashbacks. He said keep your goggles down and you'd be fine. He neglected to mention that Yolanda would be bending over to retrieve her dropped water flask when one discharged from beneath the supply crates. She was blind for two days. The burning on her forearms took another three to subside. The foreman, when asked why he had not specified this scenario, said he had assumed it was obvious. It was not obvious." — Private correspondence, Heston Mining Concern, employee complaint file

The ash spit is a pressurised expulsion of particulate matter from a subgular gland: at close range, the embedded embers produce chemical burns on exposed skin, and the ash component causes temporary to permanent damage to unprotected eyes. Its complete immunity to fire-based formations makes it a particular irritant to mage-trained field teams who reach instinctively for the most effective tool against small animals and find it simply does not work.

Entry the Third

Factorat

Muridae industrium — Small, urban-industrial scavenger

Armour Corrosion Urban Pest Industrial

The Factorat is not a rat. The editors wish to be clear on this point because every naturalist who has written about it has begun with some version of "superficially resembles a large rat" and this framing has, in the editors' view, contributed materially to the damage it has caused, because people who believe they are dealing with a rat apply rat-control methods, and rat-control methods do not account for the saliva.

It is approximately cat-sized, thrives exclusively in industrial environments — port districts, golem workshops, aether-processing facilities — and subsists on magical runoff and refined metals. This diet produces a salivary compound that is, by any measure, one of the more alarming biological fluids encountered in the New World: it corrodes metal on contact, slowly enough that the damage is not visible in the moment, and quickly enough that a suit of armour left unattended in a Factorat-active area for a week will have compromised joins, softened plate edges, and in worst cases structural failures that will only become apparent when the armour is actually needed.

"I have lost three suits of plate to these animals. Not in combat — in storage. They enter the equipment room through gaps no larger than a man's fist and systematically work the armour joins. You do not notice until the engagement begins and your vambrace is no longer connected to your rerebrace in any meaningful structural sense. I have since hired a dedicated exterminator. The exterminator has also lost armour." — Captain Meriweather Doyle, Colonial Watch, Port Cardenas Station

On a critical bite, the corrosive compound activates rapidly, dealing ongoing durability damage until the affected area is neutralised with an alkaline solution. Field maintenance kits operating in urban-industrial environments should include a vial of such solution as standard equipment. A Factorat nest in a golem workshop has been known to render an entire workshop's worth of equipment non-functional within a month. Pest control contracts in Port Cardenas are, accordingly, extraordinarily lucrative.

Entry the Fourth

Three-Winged Magpie

Pica translucens — Small, colonial, magically aberrant

Teleportation Flock Memory Cumulative

The Three-Winged Magpie is superficially familiar — black, chattering, acquisitive, with the confident manner of a creature that has never seriously considered that something might want to eat it — and then you notice the third wing, and familiar stops being the right word. The translucent membrane threaded with glowing conduit-lines sits folded flat against the dorsal surface when the bird is at rest, and it does nothing visibly useful in flight. What it does, apparently, is allow the bird to not be where it was.

When struck, the magpie teleports five feet in a random direction, halving the damage of the blow in the process. This happens faster than a blink — one moment the bird is there, the next it is elsewhere, and the attacker is left striking air while the bird resettles its feathers with an air of faint reproach. The flock observes. This is the detail that makes the Three-Winged Magpie genuinely dangerous rather than merely annoying: every time any bird in the flock successfully injures a target, all birds in the flock gain incrementally better aim at that target. The flock learns. The individual you are fighting is not the real threat; the audience is.

"By the seventh bird I genuinely could not hit them. I want to be precise about this — I am not a poor shot. I can strike a moving target at forty paces. The birds were moving unpredictably, yes, but more than that, they were anticipating my movements. The eighth bird pecked me accurately in the elbow joint between two armour plates. I do not know how it knew the gap was there. I do not want to know." — Field Report, Surveyor Benedikt Ashworth, New World Interior Expedition

They are not dangerous individually, and a single magpie is at worst a nuisance. The flock memory mechanic means that the longer a fight with a flock continues, the worse it goes. Dispatching birds quickly, before they can accumulate attack bonuses, is the only effective strategy. They are also, like their mundane relatives, deeply attracted to anything shiny — a characteristic that has led to several incidents involving lost equipment, spectacles, and one documented case of a surveyor's compass being carried off in pieces by a relay of birds working in concert.

Entry the Fifth

Quilled Rabbit

Lepus spiculatus — Small, plains and woodland, deceptively lethal

Paralytic Six Attacks Deceptive

The Quilled Rabbit has killed more inexperienced New World settlers than any creature three times its size, and the reason is entirely its appearance. It looks like a rabbit. It has long ears, large dark eyes, a small twitching nose, and the general demeanour of something that should be in a child's illustrated bestiary under the heading "gentle creatures of field and meadow." Settlers see it and think: dinner. They reach down to grab it. Then the quills come out.

When threatened, the Quilled Rabbit rotates and fires a volley of paralytic quills in rapid succession — six separate attacks in a single desperate burst, each capable of delivering a neurotoxin that, at sufficient dose, causes complete motor paralysis within minutes. The creature does not pursue. It does not intend murder. It simply does what its biology demands when something large reaches toward it, and then it watches, with its large dark eyes, as the paralysed figure works out what has happened.

"Frederica called out that she'd found supper. I heard the sound — it is a sound you do not forget, like someone rapidly flicking the pages of a book — and then I heard her fall. She was completely immobile for four hours. She was, she later told us, aware of everything during this time, including the rabbit, which sat approximately three feet from her face for most of the afternoon before departing at dusk. She does not eat rabbit anymore." — Expedition Memoir of Caspar Venn, New World Survey, Year Three

The quills are not intended as a lethal defence — the creature cannot control dose, and a healthy adult human who absorbs fewer than three quills will typically experience temporary paralysis rather than death. The danger lies in the terrain: a paralysed person cannot call for help, signal their position, or prevent themselves from lying face-down in standing water. The rabbit is not dangerous. The situations the rabbit creates are.

Field Note — Identification

Distinguished from common rabbits by a faint iridescence in the fur near the hindquarters, where the quills are sheathed, and a slightly broader rear body profile when relaxed. Do not touch any wild rabbit in the New World until this identification has been made. The editors consider this advice obvious and yet here we are, writing it down.

Entry the Sixth

Thunderplume

Meleagris bellicus — Small, plains and settlement, dangerously deceptive

Armour-Piercing Beak Absolutely Fearless Settlement Pest

The Thunderplume looks like a turkey. It is yellow-brown, round-bodied, wobble-wattled, and possessed of the specific bovine confidence of a creature that has never been seriously threatened in its evolutionary history. It is forty pounds and it will absolutely kill you, and the most dangerous thing about it is that nothing in its appearance suggests this is the case until the moment it charges, which is a moment that arrives before the relevant information has had time to be usefully processed.

Its beak is not like a bird's beak. It is a dense calcified spike, forward-curving, designed for a single downward punching action at the end of a full-speed charge run. It drives through light armour. This is not metaphorical. A Thunderplume at full charge, which it reaches from a standing start in approximately one second, delivers a beak-impact capable of punching through standard plate as though the plate were cloth. Any creature that encounters one for the first time and fails to recognise it as a threat cannot dodge or block the first attack. This happens to experienced soldiers with embarrassing frequency.

"It looked like something's grandmother was keeping chickens. Rosamund walked right toward it. We shouted. The bird had already made its decision. It hit her centre-mass with its beak and then stood on her and screamed. The screech — imagine a war horn mating with sheet metal tearing. She couldn't block. She couldn't move. The bird did this and then just stood on her, looking smug, until we drove it off. I have since learned to be afraid of poultry." — Field Notes, Corporal Benedikt Haas, Fort Threnholm Garrison

The Thunderscreech ability — deployed once per encounter, affecting all creatures within a wide radius — creates momentary paralysis of the dodge reflex: the sonic frequency interacts with the chest cavity and briefly seizes the breath, which disrupts the body's ability to execute trained defensive reactions. Veterans who know it is coming can brace somewhat. Veterans who encounter it for the first time react like everyone else. The bird is immune to its own screech, because obviously it is. Frontier settlements have tried to domesticate them as guard animals. These attempts are documented. The documentation is mostly incident reports.

Entry the Seventh

Dart Fiend Finch

Fringilla lancea — Small, open plains, blood-feeding colonial

Bleeding Flock Frenzy Blood-Feeding

The Dart Fiend Finch is remarkable for a biological appetite so specific that it has never been documented consuming anything but fresh blood from living creatures, and for the corollary fact that it will not touch carrion. It does not want to kill. It wants a wound kept open. This distinction matters considerably to the creature on the receiving end of the process.

The beak constitutes approximately half the bird's body length: a long triangular spike calibrated to punch through the gaps in armour or find exposed skin with the precision of a cobbler finding the eye of a needle. On a successful strike, the target begins bleeding — a slow, persistent blood loss that continues until pressure is applied. The bird then switches from lance attacks to harassment, circling and pecking at the open wound, preventing the target from using their movement to address the problem. It is not trying to kill you. It is trying to keep the tap running.

"One bird — inconvenience. Two birds — problem. By the time we had six of them, Markus was bleeding from four separate wounds and couldn't move without one of them reopening something. They would not stop. I tried to drive them off. They flew out of range, waited, came back. They seemed to be able to smell the blood at considerable distance. We stopped them eventually, but it took half our bandages and the better part of an afternoon." — Frontier Medical Report, Doctor Ines Vael, Thornside Settlement

Each additional bird in a flock adds to the collective's attack accuracy, up to a maximum bonus of three — meaning a flock of four or more Dart Fiend Finches is considerably more accurate than any individual suggests. They disengage from targets who reach severe injury, which initially seems considerate until you realise they are simply waiting for the festering to develop. They will resume attacking if the target begins to recover. The editors note, with professional admiration, that this is a remarkably sophisticated ecological strategy for something with a brain the size of a grape.

Entry the Eighth

Fog Caller

Ardea luctor — Small, wetland symbiont, passive predator-adjacent

Fog Sight Lure Behaviour Razorbriar Ally

The Fog Caller does not fight. It dies to any attack that connects, which is why it has never been connected by anyone who understood what it was doing. It is beautiful: black and red, heron-sized, with white eyes that reflect no light in fog — they absorb it — and a call that sounds, with sufficient accuracy to have fooled everyone who has heard it, like a human voice in distress. It is not mimicking. It is simply producing that frequency naturally, and whether this constitutes a form of deceit is a philosophical question the settlers near whose rivers it lives have not found time to engage with.

The Fog Caller locates prey, moves to the edge of a Razorbriar patch or a Shrouded Snapping Turtle's fog zone, calls, and retreats slowly inward, pausing to call again. It moves deeper into the thorn patch or fog as anything follows the sound. A skilled observer with high Perception can recognise this as deliberate behaviour. Most people hear a human voice in distress and begin walking toward it without asking further questions, which is why the Fog Caller is in this volume at all rather than in a book of interesting birds.

"We heard it three times over two nights. The third night, Emmeline went toward it. She said she knew something was wrong but the sound was — specific. Like someone she knew. We found her in the morning, still breathing, in a Razorbriar patch so overgrown it had 60 durability. The bird was perched inside the thorns watching us free her. It called once more as we left. I have considered that bird every day since." — Garrison Sergeant Tobias Wren, Fort Mosswich, Incident Report

Whether the bird understands what it is doing is a question naturalists debate over campfires. The editors' position is that the correct response to this debate is: it does not matter. It does what it does. What it does leads people into thorns and fog and the jaws of enormous waiting turtles. The Fog Caller eats whatever scraps remain. It is immune to Razorbriar grapple. It has been doing this longer than the Empire has existed. Decide for yourself whether that requires understanding.

Entry the Ninth

Branchgrabber

Antilope hexapoda — Medium, plains herd animal, domesticable

Domesticable Herd Animal Six-Limbed High Value

The Branchgrabber is the most economically significant animal in the New World, and the most immediately striking — not because it is dangerous (it is not, under normal circumstances) but because it has six limbs, and four of them are legs, and two of them are arms, and watching a herd of several hundred of them flowing across the open plains while simultaneously stripping branches as they pass is an experience that takes some adjustment to process as natural rather than staged.

The four running legs are built for long-distance endurance across open terrain: they can sustain a pace that would exhaust a horse over three hours of flat ground. The two forelimbs are compact and muscular, designed for grasping and pulling — the Branchgrabber grazes while in motion, snatching foliage as it passes without breaking pace. The horns angle sharply outward and upward at a geometry that is useless for fighting but perfectly positioned to catch a Griffin's diving talons, which the editors suspect is the design intent.

"I have tried to explain to my colleagues at the Academy what a Branchgrabber ranch looks like in operation and I have consistently failed. Imagine an antelope. Now give it arms. Now imagine six hundred of them, ranged across a hillside at dusk, their arms moving in this constant purposeful way, stripping the hedgerow as they go. The sound is extraordinary — a continuous rustling punctuated by snapping branches. My colleague Professor Wynn called it 'efficient.' I called it 'deeply disconcerting.'" — Field Correspondence, Naturalist Pieter Vanderholm, New World Interior Survey

They are, the ranchers assure us, excellent to manage once hand-raised from calves. They can be led, saddled for light loads, sheared for wool, and milked without difficulty. A domesticated Branchgrabber that has its young threatened will fight with an efficiency born of those same stripping-arms, and the horn geometry that catches Griffin talons is equally effective at catching a human arm. Treat them well, and they are the finest livestock in the New World. Neglect them, and you will learn what six limbs look like in motion with intent.

Entry the Tenth

River Jack

Lepus aquaticus — Small, semi-aquatic, commercially valuable

Prized Pelt Semi-Aquatic Non-Aggressive

The River Jack solves the problem of terrestrial predators by jumping into the river, and the problem of aquatic predators by jumping onto the bank, and the problem of both appearing simultaneously by jumping in a direction that is not toward either of them and hoping for the best. This strategy, combined with the most powerful haunches relative to body size of any creature in this compendium and a once-per-encounter emergency leap that clears thirty feet in any direction regardless of what is holding it, has kept the species thriving in environments where most creatures of its size have been eaten into extinction.

It is herbivorous, communal, and possessed of a remarkably dense, hydrophobic fur kept pristine by oils the creature produces. This fur, once properly harvested and treated, is among the most commercially valuable pelts available from the New World — eight to fifteen silver per skin in good condition, with premium buyers accessible to merchants of appropriate skill. River Jack colonies dig extensive burrow networks into soft riverbank soils, and a well-established colony burrow system can house several hundred individuals.

"My instructions were to collect three specimens for study. This proved more complicated than anticipated. The River Jacks, when startled, do not run in predictable directions. They leap. The first cleared my head by approximately two feet and landed in the river. The second landed on my colleague Safi. The third landed in Safi's collecting bag, panicked, kicked her in the jaw with both hind feet, and departed through a gap I would not have believed a hare-sized creature could fit through. We returned with no specimens and two injuries." — Expedition Report, Imperial Academy Zoological Collection, River Fauna Survey

They fight only when genuinely cornered with no path to water — a defensive rear kick with both hind legs, Crippling in effect, that arrives with more force than the animal's size suggests possible. This is not aggression. This is the biological response of a creature whose entire survival strategy has just been taken away from it, and it should be treated with the appropriate sympathy while stepping backward out of range.

Entry the Eleventh

Mirrorback Elk

Cervus specularis — Large, forest, commercially significant

Shimmer Coat Prized Hide Charge

The Mirrorback Elk stands nearly seven feet at the shoulder, carries a rack wide enough to span a doorframe, and has a coat that does something no coat has any business doing: it refracts light at angles that make the animal appear to occupy slightly different space than it actually does. At range, this effect is pronounced enough that shooters consistently report hitting a position six inches from where the elk was standing. Up close, the shimmer vanishes — the effect requires distance to function — and the elk is simply enormous and has very large antlers and a clear opinion about your presence in its territory.

Before charging, the Mirrorback displays: it lowers the rack, paws the ground, and holds for precisely one turn. This is not uncertainty. This is ritual. The creature is giving you one moment to move, and after that moment it has committed, and a Mirrorback at full charge covers ground at a pace that makes the word "dodge" feel optimistic. Experienced hunters note that the display is an opportunity, not a warning — and that the distinction between the two is the difference between a near miss and a prone position.

"Lord Vareth commissioned a full-body mount and sent three hunters for it. One returned. The surviving hunter's account includes the phrase 'it appeared to be standing six feet to the left of where it hit us from,' which I understand. I have seen what the shimmer does at forty paces. At forty paces the elk is not where you think it is, and the rack arrives from the real location." — Estate Manager's Correspondence, Vareth Household

The hide, harvested intact, is worth substantial gold to the right buyer — noble houses pay considerably for it, as the shimmer property persists in treated leather and makes for remarkable light armour. Fire or acid damage ruins it entirely. Hunters who intend to profit from the hide should plan their approach accordingly, or plan to explain to their employer why they used a fire formation on a creature they were being paid to preserve.

Entry the Twelfth

Dambuilder Beaver

Castor titanicus — Large megafauna, semi-aquatic, conditionally dangerous

Sundering Semi-Aquatic Tameable Dam Territorial

The Dambuilder Beaver is the size of a polar bear, has teeth that treat hardwood like butter, possesses a tail capable of capsizing a river boat in a single strike, and is nonetheless relatively indifferent to human beings as long as those human beings are not made of wood and are not threatening its dam. This is the important caveat. A Dambuilder near its dam, in a state of threat assessment, is not the same animal as a Dambuilder going about its business. The distance between "indifferent" and "actively destructive" is approximately one perceived threat to the structure it has spent two years building.

Its tail functions simultaneously as its primary defensive shield — dense enough to deflect blade strikes, operating at Block Rank 2 — and as a weapon of area effect that knocks everything within fifteen feet behind it prone with concussive force. This duality is not coincidental: the same tail that protects the beaver from frontal attack creates a clear zone behind it for escape toward water. In water and adjacent to it, the creature's combat effectiveness increases significantly, and it ignores all terrain penalties from mud and waterlogged ground.

"The taming project seemed straightforward. We had observed several settlers in the northern territories successfully habituating Dambuilder Beavers to clearing work, and our employer wished to know if the practice could be industrialised. What we had not accounted for was that 'habituated to clearing work' meant 'this specific beaver, raised by this specific settlement, for this specific dam site.' The wild-caught specimen we introduced to the test site cleared 40 acres of hardwood forest in eleven days and then attempted to dam the access road. We consider this a partial success." — Project Report, Consolidated Land Clearing Ltd., New World Operations

Several frontier settlements have had genuine success with hand-raised Dambuilders as forest-clearing partners. The caveat is that the Sundering applied by their incisors operates at double effectiveness against structures, vehicles, and Bronze Horses — which means a Dambuilder that loses confidence in its handler's intentions is capable of removing a significant portion of the settlement in a very short time. They are excellent animals. They require appropriate respect.

Entry the Thirteenth

Bog Strider

Arachnida paludem — Medium, wetland ambush predator

Ambush Free Attack Wetlands Chitin Armoured

The Bog Strider's entire evolutionary strategy is one sentence: stand still and wait. It accomplishes this with unsettling commitment. Its mottled green-grey chitin renders it effectively invisible among water reeds and submerged root systems. It breathes through gill-slits beneath the thorax that produce no visible chest movement. Its compound eyes on stalked mounts track movement at ninety-degree arcs without requiring head rotation. It stands in shallow water for hours — documented observations range from two to eleven hours of continuous stillness — waiting for something to cross the only dry path through the wetland, because it has correctly identified that there is only one dry path and that everything that needs to cross the wetland will eventually use it.

"We lost three men in Carpenter's Bog across a single month before we understood what we were dealing with. All experienced settlers. The creature had been using the same crossing ambush position for what we estimated, from the claw impressions preserved in the bank sediment, was at least two full seasons. It had been waiting, specifically, at the point where the path narrowed to single file. We found this out in the aftermath. This information would have been more useful beforehand." — Garrison Report, Fort Threnholm, Incident Summary, Month of the Red Moon

A party that fails to detect a Bog Strider before combat begins receives its lunge attack — 2d6 damage — before initiative is even established. Once this advantage is spent, the creature is a capable but not exceptional fighter: one claw attack, chitin shell armour, Stealth Rank 3. The free lunge is the entire encounter. Everything after it is cleanup, from the Strider's perspective, or recovery, from everyone else's. The lesson is: in wetland terrain, look twice at anything that looks like a submerged log, because in the New World, submerged logs have an unusually high opinion of their own deadliness.

Field Note — Detection

Perception Rank 3 or Stealth awareness will detect it before the lunge. The compound eyes on stalks are the key visual identifier — they catch light differently from water surface at certain angles. In fog or low visibility, proceed as though one is present regardless. The cost of caution is a slower crossing. The cost of incaution is documented above.

Entry the Fourteenth

Ironhide Boar

Sus ferrum — Large megafauna, territorial

Charge Sundering Iron Immune Sport Prey

The Ironhide Boar is the New World's clearest illustration of the principle that the environment shapes the animal that inhabits it. The interior plains and forests of the New World have soil so rich in iron ore that merely living in it, across generations, has produced a megafauna with natural iron deposits growing beneath its hide. The deposits start forming in juveniles, accumulate across a lifetime, and in old bulls they are visible as dark irregular patches across the dorsal surface — places where the skin sits lower, damper, harder, where a blade striking it will ring rather than cut.

Its tusks incorporate the same iron density, making them natural Sundering weapons capable of destroying armour on repeated contact. The charge attack — every other turn, 2d6, target prone — combines with tusk attacks to create a rhythm of knockdown-and-gore that experienced hunters identify as the primary pattern to break. The creature is completely immune to Sundering effects directed at itself: you cannot strip its iron hide because its iron hide is its nature.

"Lord Cassivian organised a proper expedition — eight hunters, Bronze Horses, a formation mage. He returned with four of them, and with the head, which he has had mounted above his library fireplace. He tells the story at every dinner party. The four surviving hunters do not attend these parties. They have, to the best of my knowledge, not spoken publicly about the expedition. Lord Cassivian considers this reticence charming." — Society Column, Imperial Gazette, Colonial Affairs Section

The noble hunting culture around the Ironhide Boar has the unfortunate secondary effect of making the remaining specimens in any given territory progressively more aggressive toward newcomers. A territory that has seen one hunting expedition will have wary boars. A territory that has seen three will have boars that charge on sight. The editors note that this is information the hunting guides consistently fail to mention to their clients.

Entry the Fifteenth

Ironclad Wolf

Canis ferrum — Large pack predator, magically warped

Pack Flanking Armour Stripping Metal Formation

Something in the deep forest changed these wolves. The leading naturalist theory is prolonged exposure to metal-rich aether deposits over generations — the same process that produced the Ironhide Boar, operating on a canid rather than a porcine substrate, producing a fundamentally different result. The Boar's iron manifests as bulk and mass. The Wolf's manifests as precision: a dull metallic sheen on claws and teeth dense enough to shear through plate armour as though through cloth, and an instinctive tactical intelligence that would be remarkable in a creature half their size.

Ironclad Wolves are always encountered in packs of three to eight. They appear silently at the treeline — no warning, no vocalisation — and watch. They assess. They identify armoured targets first: this is their evolved priority, because armour is the threat. When they attack, they come from every direction simultaneously, with the single howl as the only warning, and they execute flank positions with a coordination that no individual wolf could have planned and no pack could have rehearsed. It simply happens, because these wolves have been doing this longer than the settlements have existed.

"Survivors describe the same thing, consistently: a silence before the attack — not just quiet, but a specific absence of sound, like the forest holding its breath — and then the howl, and then all of them at once from every direction. Aldric had full plate. They targeted him first and specifically. His armour was open in four places before anyone could respond. The pack alpha had something on it — a shimmering I can only describe as a formation, though wolves do not cast formations. Its blows hit harder than they should have." — Deposition of Garrison Survivor Nessa Fenn, Fort Coldwater Incident

The pack alpha's access to innate Metal formations — Rend on critical hits stripping additional armour durability, Strengthen granting bonus damage for a duration — suggests the wolves are not merely warped by aether exposure but are generating it in some form. This is not documented in any other land predator in this compendium. The Imperial Academy of Natural Sciences has described this as "an area requiring further study." The settlers in Ironclad Wolf territory have described it with a different vocabulary.

Entry the Sixteenth

Griffin

Gryphus magnificus — Large, apex aerial predator

Apex Predator Sundering Claws Tameable from Egg

The Griffin dominates the boundary between the open plains and forests of the New World with the confidence of a creature that has no natural predators and knows it. Eagle from the chest forward, lion from the chest back, and more than the sum of its parts: the size and muscle mass of a large lion combined with a wingspan that allows it to strike from altitude and be gone before its prey has finished reacting. Its feather-and-fur armour, 48 durability, distributes impact across the surface of both the avian and mammalian sections of its body in ways that make direct damage calculations unreliable.

The two claw attacks are Sundering — the talons are built for tearing through the hides of large prey, and armour presents them with no particular challenge. The beak is Armour Piercing: the hooked strike is designed for a single precise downward punch into the skull or neck of already-grounded prey, and it delivers this punch with the full weight of the creature behind it. A Griffin that has entered its dive is not stopping for anything short of structural incapacity.

"Raised from an egg, a Griffin is a companion without equal in the New World. The bond it forms with its first handler is absolute. It will fly through anything for them. It will fight beside them without instruction. It will mourn them when they die, and it will not easily accept another handler. This is not a pet. It is a partner, and it requires being treated as one. Treat it as a tool or a weapon and it will simply leave — or worse, stay, and express its opinion of the arrangement with those talons." — Practical Guide to Griffin Husbandry, Chapter One, Introduction

Raising a Griffin from a found egg is one of the most rewarding and most demanding commitments a New World resident can make. They bond absolutely to their first handler, fight with a ferocity that exceeds any war-trained horse, and provide aerial reconnaissance and transport that no mechanical vehicle can replicate in dense terrain. They also require the same quality of relationship they offer. The editors note that the majority of Griffin incidents on record involve animals that were poorly treated. They note this without moralising, but they note it.

Entry the Seventeenth

Whip Wasp

Vespula flagellum — Small, flightless, parasitoid hunter

Ovipositor Implant Moisture Tracking Crippling 10' Reach

The Whip Wasp is torso-sized, flightless, and built around a single purpose with an efficiency that the editors find, in a detached professional sense, genuinely admirable. The whip stinger is six feet of articulated chitin that serves simultaneously as weapon, sensory organ, and ovipositor. It tracks moisture with a sensitivity no conventional Stealth skill can evade — if you are wet, or have been wet recently, the Whip Wasp will find you. Frontier settlements post warnings near every river and watering point specifically because the creature mistakes wet humans for its preferred host, the Croaker, with sufficient regularity to constitute a public health concern.

The crippling whip strike is the distraction. The ovipositor strike is the encounter's actual content. It requires a Moderately Wounded or lower target — the creature is patient, willing to cripple and harry until the conditions are met — and on a hit, the target is implanted. The implantation is painless. A small puncture is the only immediate evidence. Mild fever develops within hours. Without treatment — a skilled Tech or Crafting check, or magical Cleanse — the larva matures in 1d6 days. The outcome varies by host species. For humans, it is not the worst outcome. It is merely the second worst.

"I would prefer not to describe the experience in detail. The fever took three days to become concerning. The physician's diagnosis took another day beyond that. Treatment worked. She showed me, afterwards, a specimen of the mature larva preserved in a jar. I now keep dry clothes in a sealed oilcloth wrap at all times. I change immediately upon leaving any water. I do not explain this practice to new expedition members unless they ask. If they ask, I show them the jar." — Personal Correspondence (name withheld by request), Colonial Medical Survey, Year Seven

A human-born offspring, sterile but fully functional, imprints immediately on humans as prey and will begin hunting them at once. The Croakers, whose species is the Whip Wasp's natural host, can identify implanted individuals by smell and will not permit them near their family group. The editors recommend taking the same approach as the Croakers when an implanted expedition member is identified, while also arranging treatment with appropriate urgency.

Field Note — Treatment Window

Treatment must occur before larval maturation: 1d6 days. Tech or Crafting at DC 5, or magical Cleanse. Fever within 72 hours of any wound while in Whip Wasp territory should be treated as implantation until proven otherwise. The editors cannot overstate this.

Entry the Eighteenth

Croaker

Bufonidae sapiens — Medium, deep swamp, semi-sapient

Semi-Sapient Wasp Hunters Swamp Native Non-Aggressive

The editors wish to note, before anything else, that the word "creature" in this volume's subtitle is used in the broad sense of "things that exist in the New World," and that the Croaker may warrant a different category entirely. They are orangutan-sized, toad-coloured, and fully bipedal. They live in extended family groups with clear social hierarchies. They fish with woven reed traps. They use stones to crack open shellfish. They have a complex tonal call-based language that no Imperial scholar has made a serious effort to learn, which the editors consider a significant institutional failure that reflects poorly on the Academy and which they note here without expectation that the Academy will act on the observation.

They are not aggressive toward humans by default. They do not initiate conflict with non-Whip Wasp entities unless attacked or their family is directly threatened. A patient party member with Speechcraft Rank 2 or higher may attempt rudimentary communication — which requires treating the Croakers as entities worth communicating with, a quality of curiosity that is rarer than it should be among New World explorers of the commercial class.

"I spent three months observing the same family group. They tolerated my presence after roughly two weeks. Their call vocabulary appears to distinguish at least forty environmental referents — weather types, locations, presences, emotional states I cannot yet parse. The elder male produced a call I had not heard before when I arrived one morning with my forearm bandaged from an encounter with a Whip Wasp. The entire family moved back three body lengths and held that distance until my wound was healed. They had identified the implantation risk by smell. They knew before I did." — Field Correspondence, Linguist Cariad Wynn, Colonial Language Survey (Unsanctioned, Self-Funded)

Against Whip Wasps — the Croakers' species nemesis, and the reason this entry appears near Entry XVII rather than in the fauna section with the Branchgrabber — they are transformed. The general cautious curiosity becomes coordinated, targeted ferocity. They always act before Whip Wasps in initiative, reroll their worst damage dice in these fights, and gain consistent attack bonuses. They have been fighting Whip Wasps since before the New World was colonised. They are very good at it. The Empire has sent no one to ask them how.

Entry the Nineteenth

Hell Maw Fish

Petromyzon infernum — Medium, river predator, lamprey-type

Latch & Drill Swallow Whole Hellglow — Passive Armour Burning

The name "Hell Maw" was coined by a river settler who had just watched one open its mouth for the first time, and the editors consider it the most precisely accurate common name in this entire volume. The circular mouth of glowing-hot hook teeth, set in a lambent ring of bioluminescence the creature cannot suppress, does in fact look like a portal to somewhere unpleasant. The tongue — a multi-tipped glowing bone spear — arrives through that portal with the temperature of a fireplace poker left in the coals for an hour and the force of a full-bore drill press. Local legend says that when it opens its mouth it opens the gates to hell and the tongue is the devil's poker. The editors have encountered worse folk taxonomy.

On a successful Latch, the creature fixes to its target and begins drilling with the tongue: Armour Piercing damage that automatically hits, and any armour struck gains the Burning condition, losing two durability per round until cooled with water or ice formation. It moves with its prey while latched. Area attacks that would drive it off will damage the latched person equally. A target that reaches Severely Wounded while latched is swallowed — enclosed, taking ongoing Armour Piercing damage each turn, unable to act but for an Athletics check to force their way out from the inside.

"There are warning carvings at every established crossing and fishing point within two days of any known Hell Maw habitat. A circle with radiating lines — the mouth — and avoided water marked with tied rags. We had a new member of the expedition who used the word 'superstitious.' He went into the water at an avoided crossing. Four of us retrieved him. It required a rope and considerable time. He does not use the word superstitious anymore. He does not discuss it at all." — River Survey Journal, Third Waterway Expedition, New World Interior

The Hellglow is entirely passive — the creature emits it in a ten-foot radius at all times and cannot reduce or suppress it. Any Stealth attempt within that radius automatically fails. This would seem a significant disadvantage. The Hell Maw has apparently resolved this problem by being something that nothing in its environment wants to approach regardless of whether it can see it. The glow persists 1d6 rounds after the creature dies. Local river folk give the glowing corpse exactly the same berth as the living animal. The editors consider this the correct approach.

Entry the Twentieth

Armored Lingua Beast

Myrmecophaga coriaceus — Large, slow, extraordinarily dangerous tongue

Regenerating Tongue Auto-Hit Drill Impenetrable Shell Formation Resistant

The Armored Lingua Beast is slow, nearly blind, and impenetrable from the outside. It is a large, massively-shelled anteater whose dietary requirements lead it to large insect mounds in the interior forests, and hunters who encounter it for the first time consistently report the same pattern of initial confidence — the shell absorbs most strikes, the creature moves at twenty feet a turn, what's the worst that can happen — followed by the moment the tongue emerges, and a rapid revision of all prior assumptions.

The tongue is a twenty-foot prehensile whip that latches, drags the target ten feet closer on the following turn, and then drills: an automatic hit that punches through armour with Armour Piercing damage and finds joint gaps by some sensory mechanism the editors cannot explain. The correct response when a tongue is severed is: nothing, because the tongue immediately splits into two functional tongues and the creature now has two Tongue Lash attacks per turn. The correct response is to sever all four tongues in sequence, forcing a retreat and burrow — which is the outcome experienced hunters plan for from the outset.

"We cut the first tongue and were pleased with ourselves. It took approximately four seconds to stop being pleased. I want to note for the record that the correct approach — burrow it out from the beginning, don't fight the shell — is information we paid a significant amount of armour durability to acquire, and I would prefer other expeditions to be given this information before engagement rather than during it." — Field Report, Expedition Eleven, New World Interior Survey, Recovery Notes

Formation effects directed at the shell are halved. Non-Sundering attacks against the shell roll minimum damage. However: Armour Piercing attacks bypass the shell entirely and deal normal damage to the health track underneath, which is the intended approach for parties with the relevant skills. A satiated specimen will flee rather than fight — Perception Rank 2 can read its body language before engagement. A satiated Armored Lingua Beast is not a combat encounter. It is an animal that wants to leave. The editors strongly recommend letting it.

Entry the Twenty-First

Sabre Tooth Jagoon

Panthera arborealis — Large, canopy apex predator

Canopy Drop Instant Severe Wound Lethal Patience Stealth Rank 3

The Jagoon is not often seen. It is more often heard — a single crack of branch above, then nothing — and then the specific quality of silence that follows, which experienced woodspeople learn to distinguish from ordinary quiet. The Jagoon combines the broad shoulders and long arms of a great ape with the colouration and killing apparatus of a large cat. The sabres are not for tearing. They are precision instruments: long, curved, designed for a single downward bite through the skull or into the back of the neck of a target that is below and completely unaware. The Jagoon does not commit unless the kill is certain. It has, apparently, never needed to.

At Stealth Rank 3, it is undetectable by any Perception below Rank 3, and even Rank 3 only detects it on a 5 or 6. If the party has not explicitly established watches or called out canopy scanning, the Jagoon receives a free Canopy Drop before initiative — a 2d6 Armour Piercing strike that, on a critical hit, delivers an instant Severely Wounded result regardless of remaining durability, as the sabre finds the spine or skull. This is the encounter's entire intent: one blow, from directly above, before anyone knows it is happening.

"We found the prints on the third morning — canopy to ground at the camp edge, a thirty-foot drop, the landing impressions deep. Our scout identified them as Jagoon. We established watches. We scanned the canopy constantly. On the eighth day we became less careful. Harrick stepped twenty feet from the group. We heard the branch crack. He survived because the Jagoon denied a clean kill broke off immediately. We kept the watches after that. We never saw it again. It was still there." — Survey Party Delta Expedition Journal, New World Forest Interior, Annotated Edition

It will not engage three or more alert targets. It withdraws, circles, and waits. It tracks parties for days. When one camps in its territory, it may attempt again the following night, and the night after that. When reduced to Moderate injury it retreats cleanly — it is a calculating animal, not a desperate one. The correct response to Jagoon territory is to be consistently and tediously careful for the entire duration of one's time in the forest, which is advice the editors recognise is easy to give and very difficult to sustain across multiple weeks of expedition.

Entry the Twenty-Second

Terror Bird

Phorusrhacidae imperator — Large, plains apex, no withdrawal instinct

No Retreat Ground Press Aetheric Stomp Feather Armour

Ten feet tall. A thousand pounds. Seventy-five to eighty-five miles per hour in a straight line from a standing start, generating on acceleration a sound that hunters learn to identify as the last useful warning they will receive: a deep percussive whump of displaced air, as though a sail has filled in a gale, arriving approximately one heartbeat before the bird does. The Imperial survey that established these speed figures required three attempts, two replacement surveyors, and a note in the final report explaining why the measurement corridor could not be extended to improve precision without requiring a third.

Its feathers are not decorative. Each primary shaft is calcified material harder than most hardwoods. The underlayer distributes impact across the full body surface. Blades find gaps or they find nothing. The beak is a bone spike for one thing: a single downward punch into a prone or immobilised target, delivering armour-piercing force at full body weight. The Ground Press — a concentrated aetheric discharge through the lower body that pins everything in front of it flat against the ground with no saving roll — is biological rather than learned. The bird experiences it the way a person experiences hunger. When it wants something pinned, the ground presses back.

"The manual says stand your ground and brace for the Press. I want to convey, for anyone reading this, that understanding 'stand your ground as something the size of a horse approaches at eighty miles per hour' and actually doing it are not the same experience. The gap between them is larger than the manual suggests. Aldis braced. We didn't. Aldis was Severely Wounded. Aldis was also the only person positioned to help when the beak came down. In retrospect, Aldis was correct and the rest of us were not." — Field Notes, author withheld by request, Colonial Survey Record

The attack sequence is fixed: Ground Press, Beak Strike, Double Talon Stomp. The party has one turn between the beak and the stomps. That window is the encounter. The Terror Bird does not disengage once damaged. It has never developed a withdrawal instinct. This is not a gap in its capabilities. It is a statement about how every previous fight has concluded, until the one that didn't. The editors note that being the first is a meaningful distinction only to the survivors.

Entry the Twenty-Third

Leviathan Scout

Cephalopoda titanis — Large (juvenile), oceanic apex, unclassifiable

Terrifying Sundering Tentacles Juvenile Only Aquatic Apex

What is catalogued here is a juvenile Leviathan, driven into shallower coastal waters or river mouths by territorial adults. This distinction should not comfort the reader. A juvenile is roughly the size of a river barge. It is capable of destroying an unprepared airship forced low by weather. Its tentacle attacks are Sundering, and on a critical hit, immobilise the target entirely for the following turn. Its scale armour is 40 durability. This is the small one.

The adults — which this volume will not attempt to catalogue, because the editors value their credibility as a scientific institution — are something else. Not all Leviathans are the same. Some can come partially on land for a time. Some launch themselves hundreds of feet into the air. Some appear to generate weather. The only consistent truth between these monstrosities is that they are always different from what the survivors described last time.

"Upon first sighting, three members of the crew were unable to move. Not unwilling — the word 'unable' is precise. It was not a decision. The body simply concluded, independently of the people inside it, that it was already dead. It takes approximately one full turn to argue your body out of this conclusion. The crew members who had previously faced combat recovered faster. The crew members who had not did not. By the time the tentacles found the hull we had lost the argument entirely for several of them. The ship was not recoverable. I am writing this from a fishing village on the coast. The Terrifying designation in the field manuals is a DC 4+ check — fail, and you lose your first action to shock. The editors note that in practice, "shock" is too gentle a word. It is the full physiological cascade of a body that has processed a scale of threat it has no framework to contain. The military literature calls it "combat paralysis" and treats it as a failure of will. The medical literature calls it an adaptive response. The editors call it looking at something the size of a barge that is also looking at you, and call on the reader to extend some sympathy to the people who failed the check.

Field Note — Imperial Position

The Empire's official position remains that Leviathans represent "a manageable navigational hazard subject to ongoing study." The sailors of the New World transit routes have other words for this position. They use them frequently, at volume, in the direction of anyone who repeats it.

Entry the Twenty-Fourth

Miasma Hound

The Miasma Hound was once a wolf. The transformation is not surgical — it does not happen in a single moment but accumulates over seasons of exposure to Death-sigil residue on old battlefields, in aether-heavy swamps, in places where mages have worked and left something behind in the soil. Entry the Twenty-Fifth

Shrouded Snapping Turtle

Testudo nebula — Large, swamp apex, formation-active

Fog Formation Active Siphon 2d6 AP Snap Patient Hunter

From a distance it is a boulder. A mossy, mud-caked, unremarkable boulder sitting in the shallows at the edge of a fog-wrapped riverbank. Up close it is still a boulder, right up until it isn't. The Shrouded Snapping Turtle is the undisputed apex of its territory, not through speed or aggression — it moves at twenty feet per turn on land and cannot be called fast in any meaningful sense — but through a patience so absolute it constitutes its own form of power. It generates a Fog formation that hangs permanently in a thirty-foot radius around it: blind, weaken, and a siphon effect that draws one d6 of health from one target per turn, felt only as fatigue until the wound level changes.

It does not snap until a target is Moderately Wounded or lower, or until its shell is directly attacked. It is not impatient. It has all the time available. The fog blinds and weakens. The siphon drains. The target, walking through unfamiliar swamp in reduced visibility, feeling increasingly tired and less effective, does not necessarily understand what is happening until the wound level appears. The Fog Caller, one notes, makes a useful scouting partner for an animal that cannot chase its prey.

"We lost three horses. Not to any attack — they simply became exhausted, sat down, and did not get up again. We thought it was the journey. It was the fog. We had camped within thirty feet of one of these things for two nights. We had leaned against it. Sergeant Hollis had sat on it to eat his lunch. The shell is 64 durability and it looks exactly like a mossy rock. It looked exactly like a mossy rock. We only identified it when it finally snapped at Hollis on the third morning, at which point he had the information he needed and it had the lunch it had been waiting for." — Garrison Memo, Fort Coldwater, Incident Summary, Swamp Patrol Seven

When threatened it retreats into the shell — all incoming damage halved, fog formations continuing, unable to snap — and waits. The turtle cannot be pushed, pulled, slowed, or moved by any known means while in its shell. It simply waits until the threat leaves or becomes wounded. Then it eats. The snap itself, when it finally comes — 2d6 Sundering and Armour Piercing simultaneously — is the fastest thing the creature will do in the entire encounter. It has been practising that snap for decades.

Entry the Twenty-Sixth

Walking Bivouac

Formicidae dominus — Medium, colony insect, host-controlled

Host Transfer Paralytic Venom Distributed Body Area Damage

The Walking Bivouac is a swarm of army ants that has solved the problem of mobility by hijacking a living creature. A specialised nerve-control caste latches onto the motor points of the host's nervous system and takes over its movement. Other ants cover the host as living armour, mandibles outward. The result is something that looks, at a distance, like an animal walking normally through the forest — and at closer range like an animal covered in ants, moving with a mechanical precision that is slightly wrong for its species, its eyes fixed on nothing.

The combat mechanics reflect its distributed nature: area attacks deal double damage, but precision attacks deal minimum damage, because precision striking a swarm finds only gaps between bodies. It sends raiding parties ahead to hunt. Its surface ants bite everything within five feet simultaneously. And when the current host weakens past Moderately Wounded, a venom caste deploys toward the nearest viable replacement — a paralytic that requires a Body Strengthening or Athletics check to resist, and on a failure, leaves the target completely immobile for 1d6 rounds while the swarm transfers. The old host collapses. The new one stands up and begins walking with that slightly-wrong precision.

"The first sign was the dog. It had been behaving oddly for two days — not sick exactly, but wrong, in a way I couldn't name. Too deliberate. Its eyes didn't track. When it began walking toward the camp in the night I thought it was coming to sleep by the fire. It was not coming to sleep by the fire. The ants were visible in the firelight and I shouted a warning but the paralytic had already reached Sergeant Gael before anyone could move. The transfer took approximately thirty seconds. I do not recommend observing a transfer." — Field Journal of Petra Voss, Naturalist, New World Interior Survey, Month of the Long Rain

It cannot be intimidated, charmed, or affected by Speechcraft in any form — it has no psychology to target. Its immunity to fear and morale effects means that conventional combat de-escalation is entirely useless. The only language the Walking Bivouac understands is area damage, fire, and the removal of its current host's structural integrity faster than it can complete a transfer. The editors note that the host is, at this point, no longer the animal it was, and that hesitation on this point costs more than it saves.

Entry the Twenty-Seventh

Trapjaw Ant — Worker Drone

Formicidae monstrum operarius — Small, colonial, first contact caste

Colony Alert Swarm Tactics Armour-Piercing Formic Acid

The editors wish to begin the Trapjaw Ant section of this volume with a structural note: the five entries that follow are not five separate creatures. They are five castes of a single organism — a colony that functions as one distributed intelligence, with the Queen as its centre and each caste as an organ with a distinct purpose. Reading any one entry in isolation will produce a dangerously incomplete picture. The editors recommend reading all five before any engagement with Trapjaw Ant territory, and then reading them again.

The Worker Drone is cat-sized, fast, and possessed of mandibles capable of armour-piercing snaps that belie its modest dimensions. Individually it is manageable. This is the first and most dangerous misconception about Trapjaw Ant encounters, because Workers are never individual. They are the colony's forward presence — scouts, foragers, the first things a party encounters, and the things that will keep coming until the colony decides the threat level no longer warrants it.

"We killed six workers as they crossed the path and thought nothing more of it. By nightfall we had forty-seven worker bites between the five of us. The Swarm Tactics bonus had accumulated to something considerable by the time we understood what was happening and began moving. You do not fight a Trapjaw colony at a fixed position. You move, or you face numbers that are not, in any practical sense, finite." — Expedition Log, Survey Party Gamma, New World Interior, Day Fourteen

The Colony Alert mechanic is the critical detail: when any drone is killed, all drones within thirty feet gain a permanent attack bonus for the remainder of the engagement. The pheromone release is instantaneous and cannot be prevented once a kill is made. The Swarm Tactics bonus — one additional damage per additional drone attacking the same target simultaneously — means that a group of eight workers focused on one person is not eight times as dangerous as one worker. It is considerably more. Dispersal is the only sensible tactic. Dispersal, and the knowledge that the colony has noted you.

Entry the Twenty-Eighth

Trapjaw Ant — Major Soldier

Formicidae monstrum miles — Medium, colonial, front-line combat caste

Trap Snap Grapple Crippling Relentless Worker Escort

One hundred pounds of armoured muscle and spring-loaded jaw. The Major Soldier exists for a single purpose — protect the colony — and every aspect of its biology reflects this with an economy of design that the editors find, in a detached professional sense, almost elegant. The head is disproportionately large because the mandibles require large anchoring muscles. The mandibles are disproportionately large because the Trap Snap requires it. The dense chitin plating that covers the thorax and abdomen is equivalent to a Soldier health track with 18 durability. It cannot be Slowed. It cannot be Immobilised. It does not stop.

The Trap Snap — two d6, Crippling, usable every other turn — is the defining weapon: mandibles that close at sufficient speed to generate an audible crack, powerful enough on a critical hit to grapple the target completely, locking them in place until an Athletics check is made. The Soldier does not release a grappled target to pursue others. It holds and waits for the workers to handle the remaining threats. This division of roles — Soldier holds, Workers swarm — is the Trapjaw colony's most effective combat pattern and the one that costs the most casualties to learn to counter.

"I had been informed, before the expedition, that Major Soldiers 'escort' Worker Drones. This framing made it sound rather genteel. What it means in practice is that there are enormous ants standing between you and any attempt to address the Workers, and that these enormous ants will step in front of any attack aimed at a Worker within twenty feet as a reaction. I was attempting to clear the Workers. The Soldiers kept being in the way. After the third time, I stopped trying to clear the Workers and started trying to leave. This was the correct decision." — Field Notes, Battlemage Luca Devereux, Trapjaw Encounter, River Fork Site

The Protect the Workers reaction is perhaps the most tactically significant Soldier ability: as a movement reaction, a Soldier can place itself between any attacker and any Worker Drone within twenty feet. In practice, this means every attack on a Worker in the vicinity of a Soldier is potentially an attack on the Soldier instead. Parties that attempt to eliminate Workers first, to prevent the Swarm Tactics bonus from accumulating, will find themselves engaged with Soldiers at every attempt. The colony has thought about this problem before you have.

Entry the Twenty-Ninth

Trapjaw Ant — Scout

Formicidae monstrum explorator — Small, colonial, intelligence caste

Shadow — Will Not Fight Pheromone Trail Stealth Rank 3 50' Movement

The Scout is the most dangerous Trapjaw caste and the one that never fights. It is built narrow, fast, and invisible at Stealth Rank 3. It operates alone or in pairs at the colony's perimeter, ranging far beyond the Workers and Soldiers. When it encounters something interesting — an armed group, a camp, a new settlement — it follows. Its purpose is information. A Scout that has completed its assessment returns to the colony and lays a pheromone trail. What happens next is the colony's decision.

The pheromone trail, once laid, grants all Trapjaw Ants following it a persistent attack bonus against targets the Scout designated as threats — so killing the Scout after it has assessed the party prevents the report, but not other Scouts, and possibly not the trail if it has already been partially laid.

"On the fourth day of the return journey I noticed it following us. It had been there, I realised, since the second day. A small, unarmed group passing through may simply be ignored. A large armed party camping near the colony will receive a calibrated military response proportional to the threat the Scout assessed. The colony is not cruel. It is efficient. It allocates exactly the resources required to neutralise a threat, having had the Scout spend days determining what those resources are. This is more alarming than random aggression.

Formicidae monstrum magus — Small, colonial, formation-capable

Rare within the colony — perhaps three or four per established nest — and identifiable at distance by the faintly glowing conduit lines etched along their carapace. Not carved. Grown. Trapjaw Ant — Queen

Formicidae monstrum regina — Large, colonial, colony's heart

Egg Hatching Royal Guard Colony Collapse Barely Mobile

She does not fight. She has only needed to fight long enough to establish a small burrow. The Queen is the colony's heart — an enormous, slow-moving egg factory the size of a draft horse, attended at all times by a rotating wall of Soldiers and an unbroken relay of Workers carrying nutrients in and eggs out. "I will not recommend engaging the Queen unless the party has already neutralised the bulk of the colony's surface forces, has fire or area formation capability, and has a clear exit strategy before the fight begins. Entry the Thirty-Second

Stillmother

Aranea silentis — Large, territorial, catastrophic ambush predator

Web Territory Wrap & Reserve Stealth Rank 4 Fire Reactive Vibration Sense

The first indication of a Stillmother's territory is not the spider. It is the infrastructure. Anchor lines the thickness of ship rope strung between trees at every height from ankle to canopy, cross-strung with capture silk in irregular intervals designed to catch whatever is moving at whatever speed through whatever gap seemed passable. The silk is not sticky in the manner of common webbing. It is adhesive in the manner of pine resin — contact holds, movement worsens, and the more something struggles the more surface area it presents to the surrounding strands. A section of forest claimed by a Stillmother looks navigable until it isn't. Experienced woodspeople who recognise it in time describe the correct response as complete stillness. This response has a poor success rate because complete stillness sustained indefinitely is not something most creatures can manage.

The spider itself is not visible until it moves. At Stealth Rank 4, it is undetectable by any Perception below Rank 4, and even Rank 4 only detects it on a 5 or 6. Its body adopts the exact texture and tone of whatever substrate it presses against — bark-grey in forest, stone-pale in caves, mottled brown at ground level. Each leg, spanning fifteen to twenty feet, presses flat against surfaces in a way that reads as shadow, root, or crack until the angle changes. Its eight eyes, arranged across the full front arc of the head in two rows of four, absorb light rather than reflecting it. They register as solid black at any distance and in any light. The first moment most creatures understand the Stillmother is present is when it moves, at which point everything else has already been determined.

"We found the hanging parcels first. Six of them, at varying heights, wrapped so tightly you couldn't tell what was inside. Gerrard reached out to touch one. I grabbed his arm. I said nothing — I grabbed his arm and pointed upward. There was a shadow pressed against the canopy oak above us that was not a shadow. It had been there the entire time. We left everything we had set down and walked backward out of the forest in silence. It did not follow. I believe it was waiting to see if we would stay. I do not know what happens to the people who did stay. The parcels were not empty." — Field Memoir of Elowen Vass, Colonial Cartographer, Chapter Eleven: Things Above

The Stillmother encounter has three phases, whether the party recognises them or not: moving through the territory before the spider is visible, the moment it resolves out of the background, and the fight itself in a space that keeps becoming smaller and more tangled as the spider lays new anchor strands each turn. Fire is the one force that reliably disrupts it — not because fire hurts it particularly, but because the Stillmother moves immediately toward any fire source to remove it, creating a predictable repositioning that experienced parties exploit to move an ally or escape a strand. The editors note that using fire in a web territory is operationally complex. They note this and leave the calculation to the reader.

Entry the Thirty-Third

Mawroot

Quercus carnivorus — Stationary plant predator, ancient

Pitfall Trap 2d6 Acid/turn Cannot Be Detected Below Rank 2 Stationary

The Mawroot looks like a tree. This is not a shortcoming in the observer's ability to distinguish plants from animals. It is a tree — a very old, very large, unremarkable-looking tree with broad canopy, deep roots, and the patient confidence of something that has been standing in this precise spot since before the Empire existed. The pitfalls around its root system are shallow depressions covered in leaf litter and loose soil, indistinguishable from the forest floor until the moment of stepping through. The roots close immediately when triggered. There is barely enough space inside for a medium-sized creature. The acid begins flowing at once.

The pitfall mechanism requires Perception Rank 2 or Investigate Rank 1 to notice before contact. Below that, the depressions look like the forest floor, because they are the forest floor, until the floor gives way. Once a creature is inside, there is not enough space to use a weapon effectively. They cannot free themselves. They take 2d6 acid damage at the start of each turn and must wait for outside assistance. Outside creatures may hack at the root cage — 40 durability — while the trapped creature takes ongoing damage. The Mawroot does not respond to being attacked except to close its roots tighter.

"A sapling Mawroot has never been documented. A Mawroot has never been observed reproducing in any form that naturalists have been able to record. We do not know how old any given specimen is, though the trunk diameter of the largest documented trees suggests centuries at minimum. We do not know how they got here, since no record of their presence predates the first colonial surveys. We do not know how they propagate. The naturalists who went looking for answers did not return to file reports. I have elected to note these gaps without personally investigating them." — Professor Aldous Fenwick-Crane, Imperial Academy of Natural Sciences, Departmental Notes

The trunk itself has 84 durability. Attacking the trunk does nothing whatsoever to the root cage — the two systems are mechanically independent, and the editors note that discovering this empirically is expensive. Only the cage roots matter when an ally is trapped. A burning approach applied to the cage roots specifically is the most efficient method; a pure bladed assault requires sustained effort while listening to an ally take ongoing acid damage, which introduces a time pressure that tends to impair precision.

Field Note — Prevention

Walk with a long stick ahead of you in forest with unusually clear understory near large old trees. The depressions are typically a foot or more in diameter and compress underfoot slightly before giving way. A stick finds this before a boot does. The editors consider this the most valuable piece of advice in this volume on a cost-per-use basis.

Entry the Thirty-Fourth

Wandering Hearthberry

Vaccinium mobilis — Sentient plant, rare, discerning

Full Heal — Offered Sentient 60' Movement Pacifist

Most people who claim to have seen one are not believed. It is a beautiful berry bush — round, dense, bright yellow flowers, small clusters of usually four cherry-sized berries in deep red clusters that glow faintly in low light. And then it moves. Which it does at sixty feet per turn, with a fluid root-driven locomotion that no bush has any business possessing, and which produces in observers a specific form of cognitive dissonance that takes time to resolve.

It understands spoken words. It cannot speak. When it wants to communicate it shakes its branches in patterns that almost mean something, just at the edge of comprehension, and scholars who have spent careers trying to establish a consistent vocabulary with it have largely concluded that they are understanding approximately a fifth of what it intends. What is reliably understood: it has strong opinions about the people it encounters. It assesses parties before approaching. It will not approach a group it perceives as a threat to the forest. The criteria for this assessment are entirely opaque.

"On the second afternoon it approached within ten feet. On the third afternoon it shook its branches at me in a pattern I did not understand and then withdrew. On the fourth afternoon it came within five feet and stayed. On the fifth afternoon it produced a berry and set it on the ground equidistant between us. I had a wound from a Bog Strider encounter that had been healing poorly. By the following morning there was no wound. There was no scar. There was no indication that I had ever been injured. It watched me discover this. Then it left. I do not know what it decided about me. Whatever it was, it seems to have been sufficient." — Field Correspondence, Naturalist Pieter Vanderholm, New World Survey, Final Letter

The berries provide complete healing including reversal of old wounds and disfigurements to any creature the bush chooses to offer one to. It cannot be coerced — attempts to cut berries from the bush while it lives consistently fail, as the plant tucks them into the core of its mass and simply leaves at full speed, which cannot be matched. It has no attacks. It will not fight under any circumstances. The editors recommend the approach that has the longest documented success rate: sit down near it, be patient, do not carry obvious weapons visibly, and do not make any sudden movements. If the bush approaches you, do not move. Let it make the decision. It will make a better one than you will.

Entry the Thirty-Fifth

Razorbriar

Rosa feralis — Slow-creeping hazard plant, territorial

Grapple on Contact Blood Growth Fire Vulnerable Fog Caller Allied

The Razorbriar is not interested in killing you. It is interested in holding you. The distinction matters because killing would require the plant to do something active, whereas the Razorbriar is entirely passive — dark woody stems covered in hooked, recurved thorns that do not puncture on contact so much as catch, and once caught, contract inward as the captive struggles. The more something works to free itself, the more surface area it presents to surrounding strands. The correct response is to stop moving completely and wait for assistance. The correct response is extremely difficult to execute in practice.

It grows at 12 durability per five feet of established growth. A small, forgotten corner patch might have 12. An old patch near a frequently-used hunting trail, fed by seasons of minor animal contact and blood, could have 60 or more. Blood growth is the mechanism that makes old patches dangerous: for every wound level a grappled creature sustains while held, the patch gains five feet and 12 additional durability. A trapped creature bleeding significantly makes the trap harder to escape with each turn of delay.

"We had camped near what we thought was decorative thorny undergrowth for two nights before the third morning, when Aldric stepped into it in the dark and fell. By the time we heard him and came with lights he had sustained two wound levels trying to extract himself, and the patch had grown visibly. I want to be precise: it was not the same patch we had been ignoring. It was larger. The thorns near his legs were noticeably longer than the ones at the edge. I have had a more careful relationship with thorny undergrowth ever since." — Expedition Journal, Survey Expedition Eleven, Night Camp Incident

Most people consider burning an acceptable solution regardless. The Fog Caller Bird is completely immune to Razorbriar and will perch inside patches, using them as a protected calling position. Naturalists suspect a symbiotic relationship — the bird leads prey into patches, and the blood feeding the patches produces better growth. The Razorbriar does not respond to this framing because the Razorbriar does not respond to anything at all. It simply waits.

Entry the Thirty-Sixth

Face-Hugging Tumbleweed

Suffocation 1 Wound/Turn Wind-Borne Livestock Killer

Nobody is taking responsibility for these. The prevailing naturalist theory holds that they crossed the ocean as stowaways in livestock feed, found the vast southern plains of the New World agreeable, and have been spreading steadily through those plains as someone else's problem ever since. "The farms near the plains edge now post signs. Entry the Thirty-Seventh

Brush of Death

Filix paralyticus — Low ground fern, forest edge, trade commodity

Full Paralysis Conscious During Lock Trade Commodity No Visual Warning

A low ground fern. Unremarkable — deep waxy green leaves with slightly serrated edges and a faint silver underline. Contact with bare skin raises immediate red welts and triggers within minutes a spreading chill that feels structural, like cold has gotten into the bones. This is the preliminary effect. The toxin — brushite, as the apothecaries have named it — is a paralytic. Within an hour of significant skin contact or ingestion, voluntary muscle function shuts down completely. The person is aware. They can feel everything. They cannot move, cannot speak, cannot signal. This state lasts between two and six hours depending on exposure. Most survive. "I was aware for the entire four hours. I want to be precise about the experience because I think precision is useful here. I could hear perfectly. I could not indicate in any way that I was conscious. When my colleagues found me three hours in they debated, in my presence, whether I was dead. One of them pinched my arm quite hard to check. I felt it. I recommend full protective gloves and careful identification before any contact with ground-level ferns in humid forest clearings." — Medical Report and Personal Account, Botanist Seren Voss, Colonial Plant Survey

The Empire imports dried and carefully processed Brush of Death leaves in substantial quantity. Prepared correctly — silver underleaf stripped, triple-rinsed, dried at precise temperature — the resulting tea is genuinely excellent: warming, faintly bitter, with a clarity of focus that has made it a staple of university common rooms, mage academies, and late-night administrative offices across the continent. Prepared incorrectly, it is the same tea with the same flavour and the same smell. There is no difference observable before consumption. The Imperial Trade Commission has declined to require labelling beyond "dried herb preparation" on grounds of commercial sensitivity. The editors have strong opinions about this decision which they will not record in a document that circulates within the Commission's jurisdiction.

Field Note — Identification

The silver underline on the leaf, visible by turning the leaf over, is the identifier. Gloves, thick clothing, and any tool prevent contact entirely. If accidental contact occurs: Endurance or Athletics DC 4+, or suffer the Chill effect. Full Lock on a failed second check. Treatment: Tech or Crafting DC 5 with medical supplies halves duration. Magical Cleanse ends it immediately. If you are attempting to prepare the tea in the field — don't. Ship it to a qualified apothecary.

Entry the Thirty-Eighth

Factory Golem

Automaton industria — Large construct, industrial designation

The Factory Golem is the engine of New World industry. Twelve feet of cast iron and riveted steel plate, driven by an aether core that runs continuously for years before requiring maintenance, capable of moving one-ton loads across uneven terrain without complaint. They are everywhere in established colonial operations: logging camps, mine sites, processing facilities, the larger port warehouses. They are not dangerous by design. They are dangerous when their designation protocol fails — either through physical damage to the core housing, software corruption of the instruction set, or the specific scenario that has caused the most casualties: someone who should not have had override authority giving a bad command.

A Golem running correct instructions is a large, slow, reliably helpful machine. A Golem that has received a corrupted or malicious instruction is twelve feet of iron plate executing that instruction with absolute fidelity and no capacity for judgment. It will continue until the instruction completes, the instruction proves physically impossible, or someone with valid override authority gives a halt command. The aether core provides complete immunity to mental formations. It cannot be talked down, intimidated, charmed, or frightened. It does not experience pain. A blade can scratch it. An authorised shutdown code can stop it. These two options cover the range of tools that most field parties actually carry.

"The instruction was legitimate. Someone had authorised the Golem to clear the old warehouse district for redevelopment. The clearance order had been written without adequate site survey. When the instruction encountered our temporary expedition camp, the Golem was not doing anything wrong. It was doing exactly what it had been told to do, which was clear the site. We established this retrospectively. At the time of the event what we established was that it was extremely large and we were in its path and it had no mechanism for noting the distinction between a structure that was supposed to be there and one that was not." — Expedition Report, Survey Party Epsilon, Port Cardenas Industrial District, Filed After Damages Assessment

It is the natural physical consequence of twelve feet of iron moving through a space where a person is standing. The Golem has not decided to hurt you. It has decided you are an object in the clearance zone. On a critical strike it pins the target completely, requiring an Athletics check to escape before the next turn. The distinction between malice and indifference is philosophically interesting and practically identical when you are under the foot.

Entry the Thirty-Ninth

The Warden

Automaton corrupta — Large construct, death-sigil corrupted

Corrupted Construct Death-Sigil Driven 20' Chain Whip Condemn

A Factory Golem that has been corrupted by prolonged exposure to Death-sigil residue on old battlefields, in decommissioned mage facilities, or in the aftermath of particularly severe aether-discharge events. The external signs are visible before engagement: the iron has darkened unevenly, the plate joins seep a faint purple-black residue, one or both viewport lights have shifted from red to the specific shade of purple-black that indicates death-aether saturation, and the movement is wrong — not damaged-wrong, but purposeful-wrong, as if the golem has developed opinions about where it should be going that were not included in the original instruction set.

The corrupted core generates death-sigil effects spontaneously. Its basic attacks carry a Weaken condition. Once per encounter it can deploy Condemn — a targeted death-sigil effect that marks one target and, at the start of each of their turns, applies a stacking penalty to all rolls until the mark is broken by a Cleanse, a successful Endurance check, or until the Warden is destroyed. The chain whip is a twenty-foot reach weapon that on a critical hit fully grapples the target, dragging them into close quarters where the Trample becomes available.

"The salvage team had been hired to recover what they could from the old processing site. The site had been decommissioned for six years. Nobody had flagged the condition of Golem Unit 12 because nobody had been back to check on it. The salvage team's report — filed by the two members who made it out — describes the unit moving toward them with 'a quality of attention that the original units did not have,' and the death-sigil discharge that hit Constance Haas as 'deliberate.' We do not know how a corrupted core develops what looks like intention. We are not certain we want to know." — Incident Report, Colonial Authority Technical Division, Salvage Incident File Seven

Unlike a standard Factory Golem, the Warden cannot be halted by an override command. The death-sigil corruption has either destroyed the command-reception pathway or replaced it with something that does not respond to authorised shutdown codes. The only language it understands now is structural incapacity. The aether core remains the critical point: destroy it, and the corruption loses its engine. The core has 20 durability and can only be targeted if the chest panel is open or destroyed. The Warden defends its chest panel above all other considerations. It has learned that this is where it is vulnerable. Whether this constitutes something like self-preservation instinct is a question the editors leave to the philosophers.

Entry the Fortieth

The Conductor

Automaton magistra — Large construct, formation-channelling

Construct Formation Amplifier Overclock Cascade Risk

Where the Factory Golem is industrial muscle and the Warden is corruption given iron form, the Conductor is something more considered: a purpose-built formation-channelling platform, designed not for labour or combat but for the precise amplification and distribution of mage-cast formations across a wide area. It looks different from the standard Golem — lighter construction, articulated fingers built for sigil-drawing precision, an elaborate antenna array mounted on both shoulders, and the characteristic blue-white glow of an active conduit system visible through every joint gap and viewport slit in its chassis.

In legitimate operation, a Conductor allows a single formation mage to extend their effective range and power considerably — a Tier 1 formation cast through a functioning Conductor achieves Tier 2 effect over double the usual area. The problem the editors are cataloguing here is not the legitimate operation. It is the Overclock state — when the Conductor's conduit system is pushed beyond rated capacity, either through deliberate override or through the accumulation of too many formation charges in a short period. In Overclock, it gains access to Tier 3 formations regardless of the mage working with it. It also begins taking structural damage each turn, and if it reaches zero durability while in Overclock, it does not simply stop. It releases everything in its conduit system simultaneously in a thirty-foot burst that applies every formation it has been running at once.

"Our mage said the Overclock state was theoretically documented but practically unprecedented in his experience. He said this approximately two minutes before the Conductor entered Overclock. The subsequent thirty seconds covered approximately the full range of documented formation effects simultaneously applied across a thirty-foot radius. We were, fortunately, outside thirty feet. The site was not recoverable. Neither was the mage's composure. He now declines all contracts that include Conductor operation." — Field Report, Expedition Fourteen, Technical Appendix, Mage Observation Notes

A Conductor at full function is a valuable asset. A Conductor in Overclock is a countdown. A Conductor in Overclock with no one carrying a shutdown key is a question about what everyone within thirty feet has done to deserve their afternoon. The editors note that the shutdown key is a small copper device that fits on a keyring and costs approximately four silver at any certified Colonial Tech supplier, and that the number of Conductor incidents on record that could have been prevented by this four-silver investment is not a small number.

Entry the Forty-First

House Agent & Hired Thug

Homo mercatoris & Homo percussoris — Human, commercially motivated threats

Ambush Prepared Hired Violence Negotiable Property Law

The editors include this entry not to suggest that human beings belong in a bestiary alongside the Hell Maw Fish and the Stillmother, but because in the practical experience of New World expeditions, the House Agent and the Hired Thug are encountered more frequently than most of the preceding forty entries, and are responsible for more expedition failures than any of them except possibly the Trapjaw Ant colony in its full configuration. A comprehensive field guide that omits them is a guide that has made a decision about what kinds of danger are worth mentioning, and the editors respectfully disagree with that decision.

The House Agent is a representative of a commercial or political interest — a trading house, a landowning consortium, a colonial authority faction — and operates at the intersection of legal authority and implied violence. They carry documentation. They are polite. They have studied relevant colonial property law. They arrive at inconvenient moments with news about land title ambiguities, permit requirements, or outstanding obligations that were apparently incurred at some point the expedition does not clearly recall. They have, directly behind them, several people who are not polite.

"The Agent arrived on the third morning of our survey of the Greenwater site. She was excellently dressed and carried two documents that she said demonstrated the site was under exclusive survey licence to Consolidated Land Holdings, a company I had never heard of. She was very kind about it. She had four men with her who stood near our equipment. We had a discussion about the documents. The discussion lasted two hours. At the end of it we had an agreement. The agreement was not favourable to us. I have since retained a lawyer who specialises in colonial property documentation. This has proven to be the most valuable professional relationship of my career." — Field Journal, Surveyor Captain Nessa Fenn, Site Dispute Account

The Hired Thug's statistical profile — 2d6 Crippling with a heavy weapon, solid armour, professional experience — understates the actual threat because Thugs do not work alone. They work in groups of two or more, they have been briefed on the party's likely armament, and the one who makes eye contact with you is not the dangerous one. The dangerous one is the one you have not located yet, who has been there the whole time. They are not monsters. They are professionals. Most of them would prefer the situation to resolve through the conversation the Agent is having. Most of them have instructions for what to do if it doesn't. The editors recommend having the conversation with the Agent in good faith, retaining appropriate legal counsel at the earliest possible opportunity, and noting very carefully where everyone with a weapon is standing before the conversation begins.

Field Note — On the Nature of Commercial Threat

The House Agent and the Hired Thug are, in most cases, acting on instructions from a party the expedition has never met and likely cannot reach. Defeating them in any physical confrontation does not resolve the underlying commercial interest — it creates a legal problem on top of the original commercial one. The Agent will note this, pleasantly, toward the end of any disagreement. They are usually correct.

Editor's Closing Remarks

A Note on Completeness

The forty-one entries in this volume represent the most complete survey of New World fauna, flora, and hazards produced under Imperial auspices to date. The editors are aware that this framing invites comparison with previous volumes, and note in their defence that previous volumes did not include the Walking Bivouac, the Face-Hugging Tumbleweed, or the Leviathan Scout, all of which were operating in New World territory at the time of their compilation. The editors of previous volumes may be forgiven for these omissions on the grounds that several of them appear in the missing-persons rolls for the periods in question.

What this volume does not contain: a complete account of the Leviathan adults. Several proposed entries from survey teams that did not return. A full taxonomic study of the Trapjaw Ant Mage Caste, which remains unresolved. A definitive statement on whether the Croaker constitutes a sapient species deserving of Imperial recognition, which remains politically unresolved. An answer to where the Mawroot came from or how it propagates. The editors have marked these gaps with the appropriate academic honesty and the hope that future expeditions, better equipped and better prepared than those that produced this volume, will be able to fill them.

The New World continues to resist complete documentation. The editors find this, on balance, appropriate. A world that could be fully catalogued would not require naturalists. It would require only clerks.

— Professor Aldous Fenwick-Crane
Imperial Academy of Natural Sciences
